Shorebirds... and more!

What makes a gull a gull?

Before we can jump into identifying the different species of gulls present in Chicago, we have to first know... What exactly is a gull?

The shorelines of Chicago are rich with shorebirds of all kinds, so it's important for us to know on a general scale what we're looking at. But don't worry! It's much easier than you might expect.

Don't worry too much about the details here! We just want to make sure you can pick a gull out of a crowd of birds.

It becomes a little easier! From there you can get familiar with their overall shape. But let's break it down further, just so you can be sure.

Plovers

Plovers are what I like to call the chicken nuggets of the beaches of Montrose. They're small, round-headed, and love to scurry across the beach. They have to stay on the move to keep from being scooped up by predators.

Shape: Small, short-billed, round-bodied with a flat-topped head.

Color: Sand colored to blend in with their surroundings.

Behavior: Twitchy, almost nervous. Seen pecking with their short beak along the shore to feed.

Sandpipers

Sandpipers often occur in groups of 2 or more. They have comically large bills compared to their body size because, true to their namesake... they love to pipe through sand!

Shape: Small, long-billed, round-bodied and round-headed.

Color: Sand colored to blend in with their surroundings, often with distinctly dark spots or streaking.

Behavior: Scurrying almost constantly. Seen "piping" their beaks into the sand to feed.

Terns

Terns are the most similar to gulls at a glance. However, they have much sleeker bodies and almost never extend their necks, making them look longer rather than taller.

Shape: Long, sleek and sharp birds with a black cap.

Color: White, grey and black, very similar to gulls.

Behavior: Will sit similarly to gulls, but tend to look around more often and almost never extend their necks while standing or sitting.

Gulls

The star of our show! The largest of the shorebirds in Chicago by far, these lads will group in large quantities across the city.

Shape: Large and tall, with plump round bodies and long black tails.

Color: Typically white with some amounts of grey and black.

Behavior: Content to sit or stand in large groups, but sometimes can be seen playing with sticks or trash!

Gull Traits

Great! Now that you can pick a gull out of a crowd of birds, let's talk about the parts of a gull that help you figure out its exact species.

There's a lot of different parts to a gull. Thankfully, you only really need to look at 3 main parts of this lesson.

1 - Relative Size

This one is pretty straightforward, but different species of gulls can actually vary in size very visibly. It's good to be able to notice that when two gulls are standing next to each other.

2 - The Mantle

The back of the gull is called the mantle. This part of the gull often blends in with the color of the back of the wings. Different species of gulls have different shades of grey on their mantle. Thankfully, you don't have to necessarily memorize what specific hexcode color the mantle is or anything like that. All you need to be able to do is tell if it's particularly light grey or dark grey... especially compared to other gulls it may be next to!

3 - The Bill

The beak is also known as a bill. These terms are used interchangibly in the birdwatching community. Some bills are longer than others, but what's most important (and most observable) is what's on the bill!

Gonys Spot

The gonys spot is a large red bump found on the bill, towards the end and on the lower half.

Ring

The ring, also known as a band, looks like someone has painted a line all the way around the edge of the bill.

It should be noted that there are other useful methods of identification, such as leg color, tail color, eyerings, and primary feather coloration. However, these are not always visible depending on the gull's current position and have complex variations that are less reliable for beginner birders.

Extra Note

Gulls can vary in appearance drastically depending on their age. For the sake of this lesson, we'll just focus on adult gulls. But just so you know, gulls who are not fully grown and ready to have kids will have patchy brown splotches on them. The more brown they have, the closer to their juvenile years they are.

Chicago's Locals

Before we get to identifying, let's talk about the actual species you're likely to see here in chicago.

The American Herring Gull

Large and imposing, these gulls are found all across America in large quantities. They patrol shorelines and are competitive scavengers, rarely seen alone.

The Ring-Billed Gull

Smaller, cuter and sociable, the Ring-Billed Gulls often flock in large quantites near sources of water or food. Similar to the American Herring Gull, they are found all across America in large quantities, though their flocks tend to be much larger than the American Herring Gulls in Chicago. They are named after the iconic black ring that goes around the tip of their bill.

Tertiary Species

There are many other species of gulls that may appear in the Chicago area. However, most other gulls are typically fairly rare compared to the American Herring Gull and the Ring-Billed Gull. So what's the best way to check if a gull you're looking at might be rare?

Using eBird

Birdwatching is one of the most well-documented and constant observational activities in Chicago. Chances are, rare gulls in the area have already been documented by other observers!

Identification Steps

Now for the fun part - the actual identification!

We now know the main traits that we'll be looking at per gull, as well as the typical species found in Chicago. So let's put those together into an actual identification process!

We'll start with the most foolproof methods, then move towards more specific steps reserved for edge-case scenarios.

Step 1: Compare Size

Let's assume you're looking at a flock of gulls in Chicago. If you look closely, you'll quickly see that there are two distinct types of gulls, varying by size.

The larger gulls (highlighted in blue) are American Herring Gulls.

The smaller gulls (highlighted in red) are Ring-Billed Gulls.

Wait... it's that easy?

Pretty much! As far as Chicago goes, you're only going to see those two species in extremely high quantities together. So if you're seeing big groups of big gulls and small gulls, all with similarly colored mantles, it's almost guaranteed that the big ones are American Herring Gulls, and the small ones are Ring-Billed Gulls. But just in case, let's check some other traits too.

Step 2: Bill Details

Bill details are a great way of double-checking that you're looking at an American Herring Gull or a Ring-Billed Gull. This is especially useful if you're seeing one on its own and don't have anything to compare the size to.

American Herring Gulls have a bigger bill with a large red gonys spot.

Ring-Billed Gulls have a black "ring" around the end of their bill.

Above is a great example of an American Herring Gull standing behind a Ring-Billed Gull. You might notice that the American Herring Gull has a gonys spot on its lower bill, while the Ring-Billed Gull has a black ring around its bill.

Be careful! Step 2 has some exceptions for younger gulls.

Above is a young American Herring Gull. You might notice it has black markings on its beaks that are very similar to a Ring Billed Gull. The brown streaking on its head and body indicate this is still a young gull, which means it has some variations in its bill appearance. Still, even without the red you can still see a clear bump on the gonys. At this age, the American Herring Gull is still much larger than any Ring-Billed Gull.

Step 3: Mantle Color

So we've seen that Ring-Billed Gulls and American Herring Gulls have very similar pale-grey mantles. But what happens if you spot one or two gulls with an extremely different colored mantle?

Congratulations! You've spotted one of the rarer gulls that we sometimes get in the Chicago area; usually around the winter months.

If it's a similar size to the American Herring Gulls surrounding it and it has a very dark grey mantle, it's likely a Lesser Black-Backed Gull.

If it's much larger than the American Herring Gulls surrounding it and has a very dark grey mantle, it's likely a Great Black-Backed Gull

If it's larger than the American Herring Gulls surrounding it and has a very pale grey back (almost white), it's likely to be a Glaucous Gull.

Step 4: Double check your reports!

Whenever you see something outside of the typical Big Two, you should definitely check your identifications! Use eBird, or even strike up a conversation with any birders that might be nearby (usually you can tell because they'll have binoculars with them).

Like with any data, peer-review is essential... especially when your findings might be rare!
